# Discharge Protocol

This document describes the bedside discharge workflow for completing
the WHO Counter-Referral Form, aligned with the WHO IMAI framework and
NICE NG94.

## When the form is used

The form is used whenever a patient who was the subject of an upward
referral (documented on the WHO Acute Referral Form) is now being
discharged from the referral facility, either:

- back to the initiating facility for continuing inpatient or step-down
  care, or
- back to the primary care facility (community health centre, GP) for
  ongoing community-based care.

It is also used when the patient is transferred to a third facility
(e.g. rehabilitation, palliative, long-stay) where continuity-of-care
documentation is required.

## Pre-discharge checklist

The referral facility clinician must verify before completing the form:

1. **Medical stability** — vital signs within acceptable range for at
   least 24 hours; no deteriorating trend; afebrile or improving fever.
2. **Medication reconciliation** — pre-admission medications reviewed;
   changes documented; new prescriptions written; supply provided to
   patient.
3. **Investigation results** — all relevant results reviewed; pending
   results listed in the form with named owner for follow-up.
4. **Specialist input** — all required specialty reviews completed.
5. **Safe environment** — patient has somewhere safe to go; if home,
   the home has been confirmed habitable and safe (community
   safeguarding referral made if not).
6. **Carer awareness** — informal carers briefed; community services
   notified (district nurse, community matron, social worker as
   appropriate).
7. **Follow-up arrangements** — primary care follow-up appointment
   booked or written instruction to book given to patient.
8. **Transport home** — agreed mode of transport home (private,
   patient-transport service, family).

## Discharge categories

| Category | Definition | Counter-referral target |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Routine discharge** | Recovered to baseline | Primary care |
| **Discharge with package** | Needs ongoing community services | Primary care + named community service |
| **Step-down transfer** | Still needs inpatient care at lower acuity | Initiating facility or step-down ward |
| **Rehabilitation transfer** | Needs intensive rehab | Rehabilitation centre |
| **Palliative discharge** | End-of-life care preferred at home or hospice | Community palliative team |
| **Self-discharge (against medical advice)** | Patient declines further care | Primary care notified with explicit warning |

## Communicating diagnoses to the patient and family

The Assessment section asks "Patient / family informed of diagnoses,
prognosis, and goals of care?" This requires:

- Plain-language explanation of the diagnosis.
- Honest discussion of prognosis where prognostically significant.
- Documented goals-of-care decision: full treatment / ceiling-of-care /
  comfort care, including DNACPR status where relevant.
- Interpreter used if needed (recorded in the form).
- Written discharge information provided.

Reference: NICE NG94. *Emergency and acute medical care*. 2018.
NICE Recommendation 1.3.6: "Provide patients and carers with information
that they can take away on the diagnosis, treatment plan, medicines,
follow-up arrangements, and warning signs to look out for."

## Safety-netting

The form's deterioration-instructions field is the structured
safety-netting prompt. It should specify:

- Symptoms that should prompt return to A&E or urgent assessment.
- Symptoms that should prompt contacting the GP or NHS 111.
- A timescale for routine review.
- A named contact (clinician or service) for queries.

Common high-risk discharge scenarios needing strong safety-netting:

- **Possible sepsis** — fever, new confusion, breathlessness, rash.
- **Possible MI** — recurrent chest pain.
- **Possible PE** — pleuritic chest pain, breathlessness.
- **Head injury** — vomiting, severe headache, seizure, focal weakness,
  drowsiness.
- **Diabetic ketoacidosis recovery** — vomiting, abdominal pain, rapid
  breathing, ketones.

Reference: Royal College of Emergency Medicine. *Safety toolkit*.
<https://rcem.ac.uk/clinical-guidelines/>

## Pending investigations workflow

For each pending investigation:

1. Name the test and the expected result date.
2. Name the clinician or service responsible for receiving the result.
3. Name the action to be taken on each anticipated outcome.
4. Confirm that the primary care provider has been informed of the
   pending result.

Unactioned pending results are a recognised harm; the WHO patient-safety
curriculum classifies them as a "diagnostic error" subcategory.

## Counter-referral delivery

The completed form should reach the primary care provider within 24
hours of discharge. Acceptable delivery channels:

- Electronic discharge summary (where interoperable EHR exists).
- Hand-carried by patient.
- Posted or faxed.
- Phoned to the primary care provider plus written copy following.

## Quality indicators

WHO IMAI-aligned quality indicators for the counter-referral process:

- Proportion of discharges with completed Counter-Referral Form.
- Proportion of forms delivered to primary care within 24 hours.
- Proportion of follow-up appointments attended.
- Readmission rate within 30 days.
- Patient-reported satisfaction with discharge information.

Reference: WHO. *Strengthening referral systems in community health
care: a tool for managers*. Geneva: WHO; 2020.
<https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240003972>

## Self-discharge / discharge against medical advice

If the patient self-discharges:

1. Capacity assessment under the Mental Capacity Act 2005 (UK) or
   equivalent local law.
2. Risks explained and documented.
3. Form completed with "Self-discharge" noted.
4. Primary care notified by phone same day.
5. Written safety-netting handed to patient.

Reference: Mental Capacity Act 2005 (c. 9).
<https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2005/9/contents>
