# eye prescription

An eye prescription is a document provided by your optometrist after an eye test, detailing the specific correction your vision needs. It includes numbers and symbols that represent how strong your lenses need to be and whether you require additional corrections for astigmatism or reading.

Your prescription ensures that your glasses are perfectly tailored to your eyesight, helping you see clearly and comfortably.

Breaking Down the Key Parts of Your Eye Prescription

1. SPH (Sphere) – Your Basic Prescription Power
   SPH stands for “sphere” and measures the strength of your lenses in diopters (D).

A negative (-) number means you are short-sighted (myopic), meaning you struggle to see distant objects clearly.
A positive (+) number means you are long-sighted (hyperopic), meaning close-up objects appear blurry.

2. CYL (Cylinder) & Axis – Astigmatism Correction
   CYL (Cylinder) and Axis values appear if you have astigmatism, a condition where your cornea is irregularly shaped, causing blurry vision at all distances.

CYL measures the severity of astigmatism.
Axis (0° to 180°) indicates the orientation of the astigmatism correction.
Not all prescriptions include CYL and Axis, but if yours does, it means your lenses will be specifically designed to compensate for the irregular curvature of your eye.

3. ADD (Addition) – For Reading and Multifocal Lenses
   If you see ADD on your prescription, it means you need an additional correction for reading or near vision.

This is common for people over 40 who develop presbyopia (difficulty focusing on close objects).
It is used in bifocal or progressive lenses to provide clear vision at different distances. 4. Prism & Base – Correcting Eye Alignment Issues
A prism correction is sometimes added to help eye alignment problems (such as double vision).

Prism power (measured in prism diopters) helps correct the way light enters your eyes.
Base direction (in, out, up, or down) determines how the prism helps align your vision.

Common Questions About Eye Prescriptions

1. How often should I update my prescription?
   It’s recommended to have an eye exam every two years, or sooner if you experience headaches, blurry vision, or eye strain.

2. Can I use my glasses prescription for contact lenses?
   No, glasses and contact lenses require different prescriptions. A contact lens prescription includes additional details, such as base curve and diameter.

3. What happens if my prescription is incorrect?
   Wearing incorrectly prescribed glasses can cause eye strain, dizziness, and discomfort. If you feel your glasses aren’t working well, book an advanced eye exam with us to get an accurate prescription.
