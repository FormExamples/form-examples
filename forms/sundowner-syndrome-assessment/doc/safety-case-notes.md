# Safety case notes — sundowner / BPSD assessment

Behavioural change in an older person is a medical event until proven
otherwise. The most important diagnostic distinction is
**delirium vs dementia BPSD**.

## Exclude delirium first

NICE CG103 / NG223 — any acute behavioural change in an older person
should trigger delirium assessment.

| Feature                | Delirium                          | Dementia / BPSD                     |
| ---------------------- | --------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| Onset                  | Hours to days                     | Months to years                     |
| Course                 | Fluctuating (often worse at night) | Stable over a day; progressive over months |
| Consciousness          | Often impaired                    | Usually clear                       |
| Attention              | Markedly impaired                 | Variable; often relatively spared early |
| Reversibility          | Often reversible if cause treated | Generally irreversible              |

4AT screen items:

1. Alertness.
2. AMT4 (age, date of birth, place, current year).
3. Attention (months of year backwards).
4. Acute change or fluctuating course.

Score ≥ 4 indicates possible delirium and warrants medical workup.

## Common precipitants to exclude

The Medication Review and Medical Review steps prompt the clinician to
investigate:

- **Infection** — UTI, pneumonia, cellulitis, COVID-19, sepsis.
- **Dehydration / electrolyte imbalance** — hyponatraemia, hypercalcaemia,
  uraemia.
- **Constipation / urinary retention** — frequently overlooked.
- **Pain** — under-recognized in dementia; consider Abbey Pain Scale.
- **Medication change** — anticholinergic load, opioids, benzodiazepines,
  steroids, antiepileptics, new prescription.
- **Hypoxia** — pulmonary embolus, COPD exacerbation, anaemia.
- **Glucose** — hypo- or hyperglycaemia.
- **Subdural haematoma** — particularly after a fall.
- **Seizure / post-ictal state**.
- **Stroke** — particularly silent cortical stroke.
- **Carers' fatigue or change** — disruption to familiar routine.

## Behaviour as communication

Where delirium has been excluded, behaviour represents communication of
an unmet need or distress. Investigate in this order:

1. Pain.
2. Physical illness.
3. Toileting needs.
4. Hunger / thirst.
5. Boredom / loneliness.
6. Sensory deficits (hearing, vision).
7. Environmental factors (noise, lighting, crowding, unfamiliar staff).
8. Routine change (new carer, moved room, visitor).
9. Trauma triggers (loud noise reminiscent of past trauma).
10. Polypharmacy / iatrogenic causes.

## Antipsychotic safety

NICE NG97 §1.7.2 and MHRA Drug Safety Update — antipsychotics in dementia
are associated with:

- **Stroke** — risk approximately 2- to 3-fold increase.
- **Mortality** — increased all-cause mortality.
- **Severe sensitivity reactions in Lewy body dementia** —
  parkinsonism, autonomic instability, neuroleptic malignant syndrome.

If antipsychotic prescription is essential:

- Risperidone has UK marketing authorization for short-term (up to 6
  weeks) treatment of persistent aggression in Alzheimer's dementia
  unresponsive to non-pharmacological intervention.
- Use the lowest effective dose.
- Review every 6 weeks with explicit consideration of withdrawal.
- Avoid in Lewy body / Parkinson's disease dementia.
- Document the indication, target symptom, and review plan.

## Falls risk

The Environmental Assessment step prompts review of:

- Rugs, clutter, low furniture.
- Lighting (particularly at night).
- Bed-rail use (cautious; can paradoxically increase injury).
- Footwear.
- Recent psychotropic prescription.

## Caregiver wellbeing and distress

The Carer Impact & Support step records:

- Caregiver burden (e.g. Zarit Burden Interview).
- Caregiver mental health (PHQ-2, GAD-2 screen).
- Respite arrangements.
- Risk of carer breakdown.
- Risk of inadvertent harm (locked doors, sedation, restraint).

A distressed carer is a safety risk for both the person with dementia
and the carer themselves; offer support, including local Alzheimer's
Society and Admiral Nurse services.

## Mental Capacity Act

The Management Plan step prompts capacity assessment for:

- Decisions about psychotropic medication.
- Decisions about admission, transfer of care, or discharge.
- Decisions about restraint and Deprivation of Liberty Safeguard (DoLS).
- End-of-life care planning where appropriate.

## Safeguarding

The clinician should consider:

- **Carer abuse** — verbal, physical, financial, neglect.
- **Modern slavery / exploitation** — particularly for isolated people
  with dementia.
- **Restraint as abuse** — chemical, physical, or environmental
  restraint that is not the minimum necessary.

## Crisis pathway

Acute distress, aggression, or self-harm risk:

- Address modifiable causes first (pain, toileting, environment).
- De-escalate non-confrontationally; do not contradict.
- Contact Community Mental Health Team for Older People or Old Age
  Liaison Psychiatry.
- Admission to general hospital is often more harmful than helpful for
  people with dementia; consider intensive community support, respite
  placement, or Care Home admission rather than acute admission where
  feasible.
- Document the clinical reasoning.
