# Scoring instruments

This form combines two long-standing functional-status classifications used by
cardiologists worldwide: the Canadian Cardiovascular Society (CCS) angina grade
and the New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class.

## CCS Angina Classification

Originally proposed by L. Campeau on behalf of the Canadian Cardiovascular
Society and endorsed by the CCS Council in 1975. The four-class scheme grades
limitation of ordinary physical activity by exertional angina pectoris.

- Class I — "Ordinary physical activity does not cause angina." Angina occurs
  only with strenuous, rapid, or prolonged exertion.
- Class II — "Slight limitation of ordinary activity." Angina on walking or
  climbing stairs rapidly, walking uphill, or under emotional stress.
- Class III — "Marked limitation of ordinary physical activity." Angina on
  walking one or two blocks on level ground at normal pace.
- Class IV — "Inability to carry on any physical activity without
  discomfort." Anginal symptoms may be present at rest.

Source: Campeau L. *Letter: Grading of angina pectoris.* Circulation
1976;54(3):522-523. PMID: 947585. The 2002 CCS update is published in:
Campeau L. *The Canadian Cardiovascular Society grading of angina pectoris
revisited 30 years later.* Can J Cardiol 2002;18(4):371-379. PMID: 11992130.

## NYHA Functional Classification

The New York Heart Association Functional Classification dates to the
*Nomenclature and Criteria for Diagnosis of Diseases of the Heart and Great
Vessels*, 9th edition (Little, Brown, 1994). It is incorporated into all
contemporary heart-failure guidelines (ACC/AHA, ESC, NICE NG106).

- Class I — No limitation of physical activity. Ordinary activity does not
  cause undue fatigue, palpitation, dyspnoea or anginal pain.
- Class II — Slight limitation of physical activity. Comfortable at rest;
  ordinary physical activity results in symptoms.
- Class III — Marked limitation of physical activity. Comfortable at rest;
  less-than-ordinary activity causes symptoms.
- Class IV — Unable to carry on any physical activity without discomfort.
  Symptoms of cardiac insufficiency at rest.

The Heart Failure Society of America summary of NYHA is published at
<https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/heart-failure/what-is-heart-failure/classes-of-heart-failure>
(American Heart Association).

## Combined use

The form records both classifications because they capture distinct
pathophysiology — CCS describes ischaemic burden, NYHA describes pump
function — and patients with coronary disease commonly have both. The
ACC/AHA Stages of Heart Failure (A–D) and NYHA classes are used together in
guideline-directed medical therapy.
